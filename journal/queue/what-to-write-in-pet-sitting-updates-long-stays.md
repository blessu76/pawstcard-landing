# What to Write in Pet Sitting Updates When Nothing New Happens

**When a stay is long and routine, the best update is short, specific, and honest: one line on how the pet is doing, one small detail you noticed today, and one routine fact.** You do not need a new adventure every day. Clients want to know the pet is settled, and a calm day is good news. The trick is making a calm day sound like what it is, rather than stretching it into filler.

If you have ever sat at a kitchen table at 9 p.m. thinking "I have already said she was happy four days in a row," this one is for you.

## Why do pet sitting updates get repetitive on long stays?

On a one-visit job, everything is new, so there is plenty to say. On a stay of a week or more, the routine is the point. The pet eats, walks, naps, and repeats. That is a sign of success, but it leaves the sitter staring at an empty message box.

The risk is not boring the client. The risk is slipping into identical wording, which can read as inattentive even when you are doing everything right. A little variation in what you notice solves most of it.

## What can you write about when the day was uneventful?

Pick one or two from this list per update. Rotate them so you are not repeating yourself:

1. **A new spot.** "She has claimed the left end of the couch as her afternoon spot."
2. **A routine she has settled into.** "She now waits at the door at 6 for her walk."
3. **Appetite.** "Finished breakfast in under a minute and checked the bowl twice."
4. **A play preference.** "Ignored the squeaky toy, loved the rope."
5. **Walk observations.** "Took a new route today and spent ten minutes on one lamppost."
6. **Social moments.** "Said hello to the neighbor's terrier through the fence."
7. **Relaxed moments.** "Napped stretched out on her side in the living room."
8. **Weather and adjustments.** "Shortened the walk because of the heat and added extra water."
9. **A tiny update on the plan.** "Two more days until you are back."

None of these requires anything dramatic. They are observations, and observations are what a client cannot get when they are away.

## How short can an uneventful update be?

Very short. A reasonable template for a quiet day:

> **Quiet, comfortable day for Ruby.** Two walks, breakfast and dinner finished, fresh water. Today she discovered the sunny patch by the back window and refused to leave it. No concerns.

That is about 30 words, and it still has status, facts, a specific detail, and a clear "nothing to flag." Resist the urge to pad. A client reading from another time zone at 2 a.m. will thank you.

## How do you keep photos fresh across several days?

Photos have the same problem as text. Some ideas:

- Shoot the same spot at different times of day: morning light, evening light.
- Capture a different activity each visit: sniffing, resting, eating, a toy, a window view.
- Send fewer photos on quiet days. Two good ones are enough.

Variety comes from changing what you look at, not from forcing excitement.

## When should you break the pattern and write more?

Write more, and earlier, when something changes. Examples:

- Appetite drops or the pet refuses a meal
- Unusual stool, vomiting, limping, or lethargy
- Behavior that is out of character
- A change in the home, such as a broken gate or appliance issue
- Anything that affects the pet's safety

In those cases, put it first, describe only what you observed, state what you did, and follow the emergency instructions the client left. Leave diagnosis to the vet. A clear, calm note on a hard day can build more trust than many cheerful ones.

## What helps you stay consistent across a long stay?

- **Keep a running note.** One line per day in your own notes: what the pet ate, one thing they did. By day six you will have material, and a record to compare against.
- **Use a fixed order.** Status, facts, one detail, next step. A familiar frame lets you swap in new content without rethinking the whole message.
- **Write right after the visit.** Details fade fast. Ten minutes later you will have forgotten the lamppost.
- **Ask the client what they like.** Some want daily detail, others only exceptions. Knowing this on day one saves effort for the whole stay.
