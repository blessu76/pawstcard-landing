# Pet Sitter Photo Updates: How Many to Send and What to Shoot

**For most visits, send three photos: one of the pet looking happy and healthy, one of them doing something specific, and one of them settled when you leave.** That set tells a client the pet is fine, shows what you did together, and proves the visit ended well. More than that rarely adds information. It mostly adds scrolling for the client and sorting for you.

This guide covers what to shoot, how to do it fast, and how to keep the camera from taking over the visit.

## How many photos should a pet sitter send per visit?

Three is a good default for a standard drop-in or walk. Adjust from there:

- **Short potty-break visit (15 minutes or less):** one or two photos is enough.
- **Standard walk or drop-in (30 to 60 minutes):** three photos.
- **Overnight or full-day sitting:** a few photos at natural moments, such as morning, midday, and bedtime, rather than a constant stream.
- **Anxious owner or a first visit:** one extra, if it helps them relax.

There is no universal rule, and your clients' preferences matter more than any guideline. Ask at the meet-and-greet: "Do you like a few photos each visit, or just one?" Then do exactly what they say.

## Which three photos tell the best story?

Think of them as a tiny story: arrival, middle, ending.

1. **The "hello" photo.** The pet looking at you, tail up, ears relaxed. This is the reassurance shot. Clients scan it first for one question: do they look like themselves?
2. **The "doing something" photo.** Sniffing a favorite tree, tugging a rope, eating dinner, chasing a ball. This is the proof-of-visit shot, and it gives you something specific to mention in your note.
3. **The "settled" photo.** The pet curled up on the couch, drinking water, or relaxing in their bed as you leave. It says the visit ended calmly.

For cats and small animals, swap in whatever fits: the cat on the windowsill, the rabbit with fresh hay.

## How do you take a good pet photo in under ten seconds?

You do not need photography skills. A few habits do most of the work:

- **Get down to their eye level.** A photo from standing height shows the top of a head. A photo from a crouch shows a face.
- **Use window light.** If you are indoors, put the pet between you and a window rather than shooting into it. Skip the flash, which can startle some pets.
- **Take a quick burst, then pick one.** Tap a few frames and keep the sharpest. Pets move.
- **Wipe your lens.** It sounds silly, but a smudged lens is a common cause of hazy photos.
- **Shoot when the pet is occupied.** A dog focused on a treat holds still far better than one waiting for a cue.

## What should you avoid photographing?

- **Anything that exposes the client's home.** Skip mail, documents, whiteboards, alarm panels, and open windows showing the street. Crop or reframe if needed.
- **Photos taken while handling the leash with your phone in the other hand near traffic.** Safety comes before any update. If the situation is busy, wait for a calm spot.
- **A pet in distress, posed for cuteness.** If a pet looks uncomfortable, put the phone away and give them your attention.
- **Other people's pets or children** without their permission.
- **Photos you would not want the client to see in their feed.** Assume every picture could be forwarded or posted.

Also ask clients whether you may share your photos publicly, for example on your own business page. Treat that as a separate yes or no, not an assumption.

## How do you avoid the phone taking over the visit?

Some pet parents may prefer that a sitter keep their attention on the pet rather than the screen. That is a reasonable preference and worth building habits around:

1. **Do the visit first, photos second.** Walk, feed, play. Take your three photos at natural pauses.
2. **Batch your updates.** Send the report right after the visit, not in pieces throughout it.
3. **Pick your photos at the door.** Choose the three best while you are still there. Doing it later means scrolling through a whole camera roll, which is where the real time goes.
4. **Keep one folder per client.** If your photos are mixed with your personal ones, sorting becomes the slowest step.

The goal is the shortest possible phone time with the best possible record.
