# How to Write a Pet Sitting Visit Report Clients Actually Read

**A good pet sitting visit report answers four questions in the first two lines: Is my pet okay, did the visit happen as planned, did anything unusual come up, and is there anything I need to do?** Everything after that is the warm, personal part. If a client can get those four answers in a few seconds, they are far more likely to read the rest and keep opening your updates.

This guide gives you a structure you can reuse for every visit, a checklist for the facts, and a sample you can adapt.

## What should a pet sitting visit report include?

Clients are not looking for a diary. They want reassurance and proof. Cover these five parts, in this order:

1. **Status line.** One sentence that says how the visit went overall. "Bella had a calm, happy afternoon walk."
2. **The facts.** What you actually did: arrival and departure time, walk length or play time, food and water, potty breaks, medication if the client asked you to give it.
3. **One specific moment.** A detail only someone who was there would know. This is what makes the report feel personal rather than copy-pasted.
4. **Anything unusual.** A skipped meal, loose stool, a new limp, a broken fence latch. If nothing was unusual, say so in a few words.
5. **Next step.** "See you Thursday at 4." Or, if needed, "Could you pick up more of her food before Friday?"

That is the whole format. Five short parts, usually under 100 words.

## Which facts should you record at every visit?

Keep a fixed checklist so you are not deciding what to mention each time. A practical baseline:

- Arrival time and departure time
- Food given (and how much was eaten, if you can tell)
- Fresh water refilled
- Potty breaks: how many, and anything notable about them
- Medication given, with the time, only if it was part of the plan
- Energy and mood in one or two words
- Doors, gates, and locks checked on exit

Notice that most of this is binary. Either you did it or you didn't. That is exactly why it works as a record: if a client later asks "did she eat?", you have a time-stamped answer instead of a memory.

## How long should a visit report be?

Short enough to read on a lock screen. For a standard drop-in or walk, aim for 40 to 100 words plus two or three photos. Longer reports make sense when something happened that needs explaining. If you write five paragraphs every time, clients learn to skim, and the one report that matters gets skimmed too.

## What does a good report look like?

Here is a sample for a 30-minute dog walk:

> **Milo had a great evening walk!** We were out from 5:40 to 6:15, covered about two loops of the park, and he got two good potty breaks. He met a beagle and did his usual play bow before deciding they were best friends. Dinner eaten, fresh water down, and he settled on the couch when I left. Nothing unusual to report. See you Wednesday!

Count what is in there: status, times, potty breaks, food, water, one specific moment, "nothing unusual," and a next step. It takes about 15 seconds to read.

## What mistakes make clients stop reading?

- **Burying a problem.** If your dog walker found something worrying, it belongs in line one, not line six. Most clients can accept a hard day; finding out late is much harder on trust.
- **Vague praise only.** "He was great!" tells a client nothing. Pair every positive with one concrete detail.
- **Too many photos.** Twelve photos is a chore to scroll. Three good ones beat twelve.
- **Medical conclusions.** Describe what you saw ("she vomited once at 4:10, then ate normally"). Leave the diagnosis to the vet, and follow whatever emergency plan the client gave you.

## How can you write reports faster?

The slow part is rarely the writing. It is switching between your camera roll, your messages, and your memory of what happened. Three habits help:

1. **Note facts at the door.** Take ten seconds before you leave to jot the potty count and what the pet ate, while you still remember.
2. **Pick your photos immediately.** Choose the three best while you are still standing there, not at the end of the day.
3. **Reuse the skeleton, not the sentences.** Keep the five-part structure, but change the specific moment every time.
