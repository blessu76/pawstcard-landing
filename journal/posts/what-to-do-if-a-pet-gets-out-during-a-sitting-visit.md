# What to Do If a Pet Gets Out During a Visit

**If a pet slips out during your visit, stay calm, call the client right away, search nearby in a safe and sensible way, and follow the plan you agreed on in advance.** Every sitter hopes it never happens, but a door left open for a second, a harness that slips, or a gate latch that fails can all lead to a stressful moment. A written plan and a few habits make the difference between panic and a clear next step.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not veterinary or legal advice. Follow your agreement with each client and the rules that apply in your area.

## How can you prevent escapes in the first place?

Prevention is the best plan. Build a few small habits into every visit:

- **Ask about escape risks at the meet-and-greet.** Does the pet bolt through doors, back out of collars, or squeeze through gaps in a fence?
- **Check the gear before you open the door.** Collar, harness, and leash should fit snugly and be in good condition. Ask the client if the pet has a particular harness they trust.
- **Use a pause at the door.** Get into the habit of closing the previous door before opening the next one, and of knowing where the pet is before you step through.
- **Check gates and fences** each time. Make sure latches are secure and that nothing is leaning on a fence that a pet could climb.
- **Watch for outside triggers,** such as other dogs, bikes, or loud noises, and adjust your route or timing if the client has told you about them.
- **Keep the pet leashed or contained** according to what the client agreed to.

These steps do not remove all risk, but they reduce it.

## What should you do in the first minutes?

If a pet gets away, work through a short, calm sequence:

1. **Note where and when it happened,** and which direction the pet went.
2. **Stay calm and use a friendly voice.** A pet that senses panic may run farther. Some pets respond to a familiar word, a treat bag, or a squeaky toy. Avoid chasing, which can make some animals run.
3. **Secure the home** so no other pets can leave.
4. **Call the client.** Do this early, not after you have tried everything yourself. They may know where the pet likes to go, and they have the right to know.
5. **Search nearby,** keeping safety in mind. Do not enter private property without permission, do not cross dangerous roads, and do not put yourself at risk.

If you are with other animals, make sure they are safe before you leave to search.

## Who should you contact?

Follow the contacts in your written plan. Depending on what the client has set up, this may include:

- The client and their backup contact
- Neighbors the client has named
- The pet's microchip registry. The client may need to report the pet as lost themselves, so ask how they want to handle it
- Local shelters, animal control, or nearby vet clinics, if the client wants you to call them
- Local lost-pet groups or community pages, if the client agrees

Ask the client before you post anything publicly, and share only what they approve. Photos and descriptions of the pet should come from the client.

If the pet is found, suggest that the client have the vet check them over if there are any concerns. Do not make health judgments yourself.

## How should you talk to the client?

Be honest, clear, and quick. Say what happened, when, and where. Avoid guessing or making excuses. A calm message might read:

> Hi Dana, I need to let you know that Milo slipped out of the front door at about 3:15 while I was bringing in a delivery. I'm searching the block now and will check in every few minutes. Can you tell me where he might head, and who else I should call?

Update the client frequently, even when there is nothing new to say. Silence is the hardest part for an owner.

## What should be in your plan before it happens?

You can prepare this at the very start of a job. Add these items to your intake form or checklist:

- A clear, recent photo of the pet and a description of any distinctive features
- Microchip number and registry, if the client has one
- Whether the pet wears ID tags
- Neighbor and backup contacts
- Where the pet likes to go, and any places to avoid
- What the client wants you to do and what they do not want you to do

If you have an emergency plan on paper, add a short "pet gets out" section to it.

## What happens afterward?

Whatever the outcome, write down a short, factual account: what happened, what you did, who you contacted, and when. Share it with the client. If the pet is recovered, thank everyone who helped. Then review the situation and change any step that could prevent it next time.

A calm, honest response helps protect trust, even after a frightening event.
