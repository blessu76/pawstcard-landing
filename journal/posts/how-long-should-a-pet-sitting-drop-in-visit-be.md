# How Long Should a Pet Sitting Drop-In Visit Be? 15, 30, or 45

**Match the visit length to the pet's needs, not the price: a short visit suits a cat with a simple routine, 30 minutes fits many dogs who need a walk and a meal, and 45 minutes or more suits puppies, seniors, or anyone with extra care.** The mistake to avoid is promising a 30-minute visit that really takes 50, then rushing everything or running late to the next client. Choosing the length honestly is both a pricing decision and a promise about quality.

This is general guidance for independent sitters and dog walkers. Every pet is different, and your own experience and the client's instructions come first.

## Why does visit length matter?

Visit length is what the client is buying, and it is also what limits your day. If you book back-to-back drop-ins, a visit that overruns by ten minutes pushes every visit after it. Clients notice when the pet is rushed, and they notice when you arrive late. A realistic length protects both.

## What fits in a 15-minute visit?

A short visit works when the routine is small:

- Refresh water and scoop a litter box
- Feed a cat or small pet
- Quick play and a check that everything looks normal
- Photos and a short update

A 15-minute visit is usually not enough for a dog who needs a walk. Sitters who offer one often reserve it for cats, small pets, or an extra midday check. It is also easy to underestimate, since the time you spend arriving, locking up, and writing the update does not stop when the visit ends.

## What fits in a 30-minute visit?

This is the standard drop-in for many sitters. A typical flow:

1. **Greet the pet and settle them** (2 to 3 minutes)
2. **Potty break or a short walk** (10 to 15 minutes)
3. **Food, water, and any medication** (5 minutes)
4. **Play or cuddle time** (5 to 7 minutes)
5. **Photos, a quick tidy, and locking up** (3 to 5 minutes)

Those times are examples, not rules. A fast eater and a slow sniffer can change them quickly. If a pet needs a long walk, a 30-minute visit may leave little time for anything else.

## What fits in a 45-minute or 60-minute visit?

A longer visit gives room for the things that cannot be hurried:

- A full walk for a high-energy dog
- A puppy who needs frequent potty trips
- A senior pet who moves slowly or needs help
- Several pets, each with a different routine
- Medications that need to be given carefully
- A shy pet who needs time to warm up before eating or going out

Ask yourself: if the visit goes wrong, will I have enough time to handle it calmly? A little buffer is a good sign.

## How do you decide for a particular pet?

Use the meet-and-greet to find out. Ask the client to walk you through a normal day, then time it yourself in your head:

- How long does a typical walk take?
- How long does the pet take to eat?
- How many tasks are there that need to be done every visit?
- Are there chores around the home, such as plants, mail, or trash?
- Does the pet need time to settle after you arrive?

Add the pieces, then add a few minutes for arrival, notes, and locking up. If the total comes out longer than the visit you were going to offer, recommend the longer one, and explain why.

## How do you explain it to clients?

Be plain and friendly:

> Based on Bo's walk and his two feeding routines, I recommend 45 minutes per visit. That gives him a proper walk and time to eat without being rushed. If you would prefer a shorter visit, I can do 30 minutes with a shorter walk.

Giving the client the choice, with the tradeoff stated, helps them decide with confidence. It also avoids the awkward moment later when they ask why the walk was short.

## What if visits keep running over?

- **Track your actual times** for a week or two. Your arrival and leaving times tell the truth.
- **Adjust the booked length** for the next stay, or tell the client now and update the plan.
- **Do not quietly absorb every overrun.** If the pet needs more time every visit, the booking is wrong, not the pet.
