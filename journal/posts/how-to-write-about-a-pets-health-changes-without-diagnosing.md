# How to Write About a Pet's Health Changes Without Diagnosing

**When a pet seems off during a visit, write down exactly what you saw, say when you saw it, and suggest a next step. Do not name a cause.** Pet sitters are often the only person who sees a pet for several days in a row, so you may notice a limp, a skipped meal, or a new cough before anyone else does. How you word that note affects whether the owner acts calmly, trusts you, and gets the right help.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers. It is not veterinary, legal, or business advice. If you think an animal is in an emergency, follow the emergency plan you agreed with the owner and call the vet or emergency clinic named in it.

## Why should a sitter avoid diagnosing?

You are not a veterinarian, and many different problems look alike from the outside. A pet that is "off its food" could be tired, anxious about a change in routine, upset by weather, or unwell. If you write "I think he has an ear infection," you may alarm the owner for no reason, or you may sound falsely certain about something that turns out to be wrong. In some places, giving veterinary opinions can also create legal and insurance problems for a sitter, so it is safer to stay with observation. If you are unsure, check your local rules and your own insurance terms.

Staying with what you saw is not a way of dodging responsibility. It is the most useful thing you can give the owner and the vet: a clear, honest account that someone else can interpret.

## What counts as a health change worth writing down?

You do not need to report every sneeze. Think about what is different from this pet's normal, which is why it helps to ask the owner at the start what normal looks like for each animal. Changes worth a note include:

- **Eating and drinking:** a skipped meal, eating much more slowly, a full water bowl that is usually empty, or drinking far more than usual.
- **Bathroom habits:** diarrhea, straining, blood, no stool, accidents in a house-trained pet, or unusual frequency.
- **Movement:** limping, stiffness when standing up, reluctance to climb stairs, or favoring one leg.
- **Breathing and sound:** coughing, heavy panting at rest, noisy breathing, or repeated sneezing.
- **Skin, eyes, ears, and mouth:** scratching, redness, discharge, head shaking, a new lump, or a smell you have not noticed before.
- **Behavior and energy:** hiding, unusual clinginess, restlessness, or a pet that does not want to play when they normally do.
- **Vomiting:** how many times, and what it looked like.

If the owner wrote medication instructions, a missed or refused dose belongs in this list too.

## How do you describe it clearly?

A good health note has four parts: what, when, how much, and what you did. Keep each one short.

1. **What you saw.** Use plain words and avoid labels. "Biscuit was holding her right front paw off the ground for the first few steps of the walk" is better than "Biscuit has a sprain."
2. **When you saw it.** Give the time of the visit and whether it happened once or several times. "Once, around 5:15 p.m., none at the evening visit."
3. **How much.** Numbers help when they are honest: "vomited twice before 10 a.m.," "ate about half the usual portion." If you are not sure, say you are not sure.
4. **What you did.** "I offered fresh water, shortened the walk, and checked the paw. I did not see anything stuck in it."

Compare these two versions of the same note:

- *Too vague:* "Max seemed a bit off today."
- *Clear:* "Max ate about half his breakfast and left the rest. He was a little slower to get up from his bed than yesterday, but he still wagged and took a short walk. He drank normally. I will watch his appetite at the evening visit."

The second version gives the owner something to compare against what they know, and it does not claim to know the reason.

## What words should you use and avoid?

Choose neutral, observational language. Words like "seemed," "appeared," and "I noticed" are honest because they describe your view. Try to avoid:

- **Diagnoses:** arthritis, infection, poisoning, "UTI," "kennel cough."
- **Reassurance you cannot give:** "I'm sure it's nothing."
- **Alarm you cannot back up:** "this looks really serious."
- **Blame:** "you should have taken him in last week."

It is fine to say, "I am not able to say what is causing this." That sentence reads as professional, not unsure.

## How do you suggest a next step without overstepping?

You can recommend contacting the vet without telling the owner what is wrong. A few examples of calm wording:

- "This is the second skipped meal in a row. You may want to mention it to your vet if it continues. Would you like me to keep a closer note of what she eats?"
- "I noticed a cough twice this afternoon. I am not able to say what it means, but I wanted you to know in case you would like to call the vet."
- "He has been limping since this morning's walk. I kept the evening walk to the yard. If you would like me to send a short video of how he walks, tell me."

If the change is sudden or severe, such as repeated vomiting, collapse, trouble breathing, bleeding that does not stop, or signs of poisoning, do not wait for the end of the day to write a note. Follow your written emergency plan, which should include calling the owner right away. A written note can come afterward.

## When should you contact the owner right away?

Agree on this before the job starts. At the meet and greet, ask the owner what they want to be told about immediately and what can wait for the daily update. Many owners are happy to hear about small things at the end of the day and want a phone call for anything involving vomiting, blood, a limp, or a refusal to eat for more than one meal. Write that decision into your notes for the job so that you are following their preference and not guessing at 6 p.m.

## What if the owner asks "what do you think it is?"

This will happen. A kind and honest answer sounds like this: "I really can't say. I can tell you what I have seen and when, and a vet can tell you much more. I'm happy to write it all up so you can pass it along." You are giving them something concrete without taking on a role you are not trained for.

## How should you handle it if the news is hard?

Some health changes are serious, especially with older pets or pets that were already unwell. Lead with the facts and with care for the owner's feelings. Avoid long explanations. Say what you saw, what you did, what you suggest, and that you are available. If a pet has died or is in serious trouble while in your care, follow your emergency plan and speak to the owner directly. A written note should never be the first way they hear it.

## How do you keep a record you can rely on later?

Write the note the same day, while you remember the details, and keep it with the rest of the visit record. If you took a photo or a short video of the symptom, attach it with the owner's permission as part of your record. A short log of "what I saw, when, and what I did" protects the pet, the owner, and you. It also helps the vet, who may ask when a symptom started.

## A short checklist before you send a health note

- Did I describe what I saw instead of naming a cause?
- Did I say when it happened and how often?
- Did I say what I did in response?
- Did I suggest a next step, such as watching, a call to the vet, or a follow-up update?
- Is the tone calm and kind?
- Does the owner know when they will hear from me next?

If the answer to each is yes, you have given the owner what they need to make a good decision.
