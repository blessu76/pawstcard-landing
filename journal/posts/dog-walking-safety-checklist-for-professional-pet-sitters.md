# Dog Walking Safety Checklist for Professional Sitters

**A safe walk is decided before you clip in the leash, so check the gear, the route, and the dog in the same order every time.** The full checklist takes under two minutes, and it turns "hope nothing happens" into a plan you can run on autopilot.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not veterinary advice. Follow the owner's and the vet's instructions, and suggest that the owner consult their vet about any health concern.

## What do you check before every walk?

Run the same list every time, so nothing depends on memory:

- **Leash and harness.** No frays, the buckle works, and the fit is snug enough that two fingers slide under but no more.
- **Collar and ID tag** with the dog's name, your number, and the owner's number.
- **The dog, briefly.** Limping, sore paws, a cough, or loose stool in the yard. If something looks off, note it and tell the owner before the walk, not after.
- **Phone and route.** Phone charged, the owner's emergency contact saved, and your expected return time in mind.
- **Water and waste bags** if the walk is long or the day is hot.

Two minutes, same order, every walk. The habit is the point.

## How do you plan a safe route?

- **Scout a new route once before walking it professionally,** at a similar time of day.
- **Know the busy windows.** School pickup, lunchtime sidewalks, and the dog park rush are all avoidable.
- **Keep one backup route** for construction, storms, or a blocked path.
- **On long walks or solo days, tell someone your route and return time.** A simple text to a neighbor or a fellow sitter is enough.
- **Note trouble spots in your client file:** the reactive dog on the corner, the unfenced garden, the intersection with no crosswalk light.

## What rules keep group walks safe?

- **Only pair dogs with confirmed compatibility.** The owner's word plus a calm meet-and-greet, not a photo in a profile.
- **Keep groups small.** Many sitters cap a group at three or four dogs, and some cities set their own limits, so check what applies where you work. The more dogs on the walk, the harder it is to watch each one, and one lunging dog can unsettle the rest.
- **Stay on a short leash in busy areas,** even for "great with other dogs" dogs.
- **If tension appears, split the group.** Two calm walks beat one tense one.
- **Skip the group for any dog you have not met.** A missed group walk is a small thing. A bad first meeting is not.

## How do weather and season change the walk?

- **Heat.** Press the back of your hand to the pavement for about five seconds. If it is too hot for you, it is too hot for a paw. Walk earlier or later, shorten the route, and carry water.
- **Cold.** Watch short-coated and senior dogs for shivering, and on icy paths slow down and keep a hand on the harness.
- **Check the forecast before the day, not at the door.** If there is a heat or storm alert, move walks earlier, shorten them, or skip one with the owner's go-ahead.

## What do you do when something goes wrong?

The plan is simple and always the same: stop, assess, call the owner, follow their instructions, then write it down.

- **The dog is hurt.** Do not diagnose. Describe what you see in plain terms when you call, and if the owner authorizes it, go to the vet they named. Write down the times: "At 11:40 on Elm and Third, Rex limped after a paw twist. I stopped the walk, walked him slowly home, and called you at 11:50."
- **The dog is off leash.** Stay calm, call by name, use a treat, and never chase a dog into traffic. If it is not coming, tell the owner immediately and search the nearest quiet streets first.
- **A dog fight.** Separate only if you can do it safely. Otherwise get to the owners, and write down the times and what each dog did.

Follow whatever emergency plan the owner gave you in writing. If there is none, the call to the owner is the first step, not the last.
