# Overnight Pet Sitting: How to Plan and Document a Stay

**A smooth overnight stay comes from three things: clear expectations agreed in writing before the first night, a predictable evening and morning routine for the pet, and short, specific updates so the client knows how things are going.** Overnights ask more of you than a drop-in visit. You are responsible for the home and the animal for many hours, so the more you settle in advance, the calmer the stay will be for everyone.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not legal, insurance, or veterinary advice. Follow any agreement you have with each client and check the rules that apply where you work.

## What is different about an overnight stay?

A drop-in visit is a short window. An overnight stay is a stretch of time that includes an evening, a night, and a morning. The pet may be anxious in the first hours, may wake you, and may need something at an unusual time. You also need a place to sleep, a way to get into and out of the home, and a plan for what happens if you are unwell or something goes wrong.

Because the job is bigger, many sitters treat it as its own service with its own terms, rather than a long drop-in.

## What should you confirm before the first night?

Put these points in writing, even if the client is a friend of a friend:

- **Where you will sleep,** and whether any rooms are off limits.
- **Whether the pet sleeps in a crate, a bed, or in the client's room,** and whether that is allowed during your stay.
- **Arrival and departure times,** and how long the pet can be left alone if you go to work or run errands.
- **Whether anyone else may be in the home,** such as a cleaner, a relative, or a delivery. Ask for a heads-up.
- **House basics:** Wi-Fi, thermostat, trash day, alarms, locks, and which doors are used for the pet.
- **Rules about guests.** Some sitters make it a rule that no visitors come over unless the client agrees.

If the client has a house key arrangement, review it in the same message. A separate checklist for home access is worth keeping.

## How do you handle the evening routine?

Ask the client to write out the normal evening. Include feeding times, the last outdoor trip, any medication, play, and when the pet tends to settle. Then follow it as closely as you can for the first night, even if your own habits are different.

Many pets settle more easily when the pattern matches home, so resist the urge to change the routine early. If you think a change would help, mention it to the client and let them decide.

Write down what actually happened. A few notes are enough:

- What time the pet ate and how much of the meal was finished
- The last potty break
- Any medication given, with the time
- How the pet seemed at bedtime

## What about the night itself?

Ask the client what the pet normally does at night. Some pets sleep through. Some pace, whine, or want to be let out. Knowing the usual pattern helps you tell the difference between normal behavior and something worth mentioning.

If the pet seems unwell at any point, such as repeated vomiting, trouble breathing, or something that worries you, follow the emergency plan you agreed with the client and contact the vet or emergency clinic they named. Do not try to diagnose. Your role is to notice, record, and call the right person.

Keep your phone charged and nearby, and make sure you know where the client's emergency contacts and vet details are written down.

## What does a good morning look like?

Start with the pet's usual morning routine: potty break, breakfast, water bowl check, and any medication. Then take a short moment to look around the home. Check that doors and windows are locked as agreed, that nothing was chewed or knocked over, and that the pet's area is clean.

If something was damaged, take a photo, write a factual note, and tell the client the same day. Describe what you saw rather than guessing at blame.

## How often should you update the client?

Agree on this before the stay. A common pattern is one message in the evening and one in the morning, plus anything that cannot wait. The evening update can say that you arrived safely, the pet ate, and the evening went well, with one photo. The morning update can mention how the night went, what breakfast looked like, and what the day holds.

Use real details. "Biscuit settled on the couch by 9:30 and slept until 6:40" tells the client more than "everything is great."

## What should you do on the last morning?

Do a final walkthrough, confirm the pet's food and water are set, and tidy the spaces you used. Return keys in the way you agreed. Send a closing message that says the stay is finished, the home is locked, and what you noticed about the pet. If you saw something the client should follow up on with their vet, say so plainly and leave the decision to them.
