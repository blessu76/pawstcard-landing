# How Should an Independent Pet Sitter Set Rates?

**Set your rates by working backward from what you need to earn per hour of real work, then price each visit type so it covers your time, travel, and costs, and explain the number plainly when a client asks.** There is no single correct price for pet sitting, because markets, services, and experience differ. What you can control is a method that keeps you from guessing, undercharging, or quietly resenting your own schedule.

This is general guidance for independent sitters and dog walkers, not tax, legal, or financial advice. Check local rules and talk to an accountant about taxes and business costs that apply to you.

## Why is setting a rate so hard?

Most sitters start because they love animals, and talking about money feels awkward. Many pick a number by copying a friend or a neighbor, or by choosing something that feels "safe." The risk is that a safe number can leave you tired and underpaid after you count the driving, the messages, the cleanup, and the unpaid minutes around each visit.

A price that works has to survive a slow week, a rainy week, and a week when a client cancels late. If it only works when everything goes perfectly, it is too low.

## What should you count before you pick a number?

Start with a short list of the real costs of a visit. Use your own figures, not anyone else's.

1. **Your time on site.** The scheduled visit length, plus the few minutes you spend arriving and settling in.
2. **Your travel time and fuel.** A visit across town costs more of your day than a visit down the street.
3. **Time spent on messages and updates.** Writing and sending an update is part of the job, even though no one sees it happen.
4. **Supplies and wear.** Waste bags, cleaning supplies, treats, and the general wear on your vehicle or shoes.
5. **Business costs.** Insurance if you carry it, any software, phone costs, and background checks if you pay for them. Check what applies to you.
6. **Time between visits.** A gap you cannot fill with other work is still part of your day.

Add these up for a typical day, then divide by the number of paid visits you can realistically fit in. That gives you a floor. It is not the price, but it tells you what a visit cannot go below.

## How do you price different visit types?

Many sitters do better with a small menu than with a custom quote every time. Here are common building blocks:

- **Short drop-ins** for a quick potty break, feeding, or check-in
- **Longer visits** that include play, a walk, or extra care
- **Walks** by length, or a flat walk fee
- **Overnight stays** in the client's home
- **Add-ons** for extra pets, medication, or very early and late times

A quick example, with made-up numbers only: if you decide a 30-minute visit needs to earn $X to cover your costs and time, a 15-minute visit might be priced a bit below that rather than half, because travel and setup do not shrink with the visit. Run your own numbers instead of copying this pattern.

Think about premiums too. Many sitters charge more for holidays, short notice, medication, or extra animals, because those visits ask more of them. Say so in your price list so it does not feel like a surprise later.

## How do you explain your price to a client?

Keep the explanation short and calm. You do not need to defend your rate.

- **Lead with what is included:** visit length, an update, and the care you provide.
- **Be clear about what costs extra** before the booking, not after.
- **Do not apologize for the number.** A friendly, steady tone tells the client the price is considered.
- **Offer options, not discounts.** If a client wants to spend less, suggest a shorter visit or fewer days instead of lowering your rate.

For example: "My 30-minute visit is $X, which includes feeding, a short walk, fresh water, and a photo update afterward. Extra pets are $Y each."

## Should you lower your price to win a client?

Sometimes a small, planned offer makes sense, such as a first-visit special or a multi-day package. Decide ahead of time what you will do rather than reacting to each question. A one-off cut made in the moment tends to become the price that client expects forever.

If someone says you are too expensive, it is fine to let them go. Clients who value careful updates and reliable visits are often the ones who stay.

## How often should you review your rates?

Look at your rates at least once a year, and again if your costs jump or your schedule is always full. A full calendar can be a sign that you are priced low. Giving existing clients notice before a change usually goes better than a surprise. A short, kind message with a clear date beats a surprise on the next invoice.

Keep a simple record of how long visits really take, including travel and messages. After a month, compare it to the time you assumed. The gap is often where the money disappears.
