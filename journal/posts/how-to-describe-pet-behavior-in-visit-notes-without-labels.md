# Describing Pet Behavior in Visit Notes Without Labels

**A label tells the owner what you concluded. A description tells them what happened. In a visit note, the description is almost always the better choice.** Words like "aggressive," "anxious," "stubborn," or "naughty" feel like shorthand, but they carry a judgment you may not be able to back up, and they can stick to a pet long after the visit is over.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers. It is not training, veterinary, or behavior-consulting advice. If a pet's behavior worries you, say what you saw and suggest the owner talk to their vet or a qualified trainer.

## Why do labels cause problems in visit notes?

A label compresses a lot of events into one word, and the owner has no way to check it. Suppose you write "Luna was anxious today." The owner might picture a pet in distress, wonder whether something is wrong at home, or worry that you were not coping. Or they might shrug, because Luna is "always like that." Either way, they have learned very little.

Labels also travel. A note that calls a dog "reactive" or "aggressive" can end up repeated to other sitters, a groomer, or a landlord. If that label came from one hard afternoon, it may not be fair to the dog, and it may not be fair to you either.

The third problem is honesty. You saw a pet for twenty or thirty minutes. A label sounds like knowledge of the whole animal, when what you really have is a short, real observation.

## What does a description look like?

A good description answers three questions: what did the pet do, in what situation, and for how long. Compare these pairs:

| Label | Description |
|---|---|
| "Milo was aggressive at the door." | "Milo barked and stood stiffly at the door when I came in. He stopped after about a minute when I sat down and spoke quietly." |
| "Pepper was anxious." | "Pepper followed me from room to room and did not settle on her bed until the second half of the visit." |
| "Oscar was stubborn on the walk." | "Oscar sat down twice near the corner of Elm Street and would not move for about a minute. We turned around and he walked normally." |
| "Bean was naughty." | "Bean chewed the corner of the couch cushion while I was filling the water bowl. I redirected her to her chew toy." |

The descriptions are longer, but they are also more useful. The owner can compare them with what they know. They may reply, "Oh, that is the mail carrier's time, he hates it," and now you both know something you did not before.

## What should you include in a behavior note?

You do not need a paragraph every time. Most of the time, one or two sentences are enough. Try to cover:

- **What the pet did.** Use verbs and body language you can see: barking, hiding, pulling, panting, pacing, wagging, freezing.
- **What was happening just before.** A visitor, a noise, a leash going on, another dog, a closed door.
- **How long it lasted and how it ended.** "About a minute," "until we were back inside," "it did not happen again."
- **What you did.** "I waited," "I gave her space," "I picked up the pace," "I skipped the park today."

## Which words are safest to use?

Choose words that describe observations and leave out words that describe causes or character.

- Prefer: "barked," "backed away," "lowered his head," "licked her lips repeatedly," "stayed under the bed," "ate quickly," "did not want to go outside."
- Be careful with: "aggressive," "dominant," "protective," "jealous," "spiteful," "guilty." These claim to know what the pet was thinking.
- Never use a label that sounds like a medical or training diagnosis, such as "has separation anxiety" or "has resource guarding," unless the owner has told you a professional has said so. Even then, quote the owner instead of stating it as fact.

A useful test: could another person watching the same video describe it in the same words? If not, you are probably labeling.

## What if something happened that really matters for safety?

Some behavior is not just a quirk. If a dog snapped, lunged, or bit, or if a pet showed behavior that could hurt someone, do not soften it. Write it down plainly, the same day, and tell the owner promptly, ideally by phone or a direct message rather than waiting for the end-of-day summary.

Plain does not mean harsh. "Biscuit growled and snapped toward my hand when I reached for the bowl. I stepped back and did not try again. I fed her by setting the bowl down and leaving the room." That is clear, calm, and fair to the dog. If you are ever hurt or someone else was, follow your own safety and incident process and your insurance terms. This is general guidance and not legal advice.

## How do you talk about "difficult" pets with new clients?

At the meet and greet, ask about triggers and what helps. "What makes her nervous? What does she do when she is nervous? What helps?" Then your later notes can say, "She hid when the delivery truck came, so I did what you suggested and gave her the quiet room." You are describing a behavior and showing that you followed the owner's guidance, which is a strong way to build trust.

## How can you keep the tone warm?

Describing instead of labeling does not have to sound cold. You can be honest and still kind:

- "Ruby was a little unsure about the vacuum noise from next door and stayed close to me. She settled with a treat and a pat."
- "Toby was full of energy today. He did three zoomies around the yard before he was ready for his walk."

Warmth comes from the details you chose to notice, not from a flattering adjective.

## A quick check before you send a behavior note

- Did I describe what the pet did instead of what I think it felt?
- Did I say what happened just before, and how it ended?
- Did I avoid words that sound like a diagnosis or a character judgment?
- If anything involved safety, did I tell the owner promptly and plainly?
- Would I be comfortable if another sitter read this note and relied on it?

If you can answer yes to each, your note will be fair to the pet, clear to the owner, and honest about what you actually saw.
