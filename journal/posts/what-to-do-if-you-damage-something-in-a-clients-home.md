# What to Do If You Damage Something in a Client's Home

**If you break or stain something in a client's home, make sure the pet and the space are safe, document what happened, tell the owner promptly and plainly, and agree on how to make it right.** It is an uncomfortable moment, but sitters who handle it quickly and honestly usually keep the client's trust. Hiding it almost never works, and it tends to turn a small accident into a serious breach of trust.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not legal, insurance, or business advice. If the damage is significant, involves an injury, or could be a dispute over money, consider speaking to your insurer or a qualified professional before you promise anything.

## Why does this happen even to careful sitters?

You work in other people's homes, often with excited animals, unfamiliar layouts, and a tight schedule. A leash catches a vase. A dog knocks a plant off a shelf. A wet paw leaves a mark on a rug. A door key snaps in a lock. Sometimes the pet causes the damage and you were nearby. Sometimes it is simply your own mistake. In both cases, the owner will want to know.

Nobody expects a sitter to be perfect. What clients remember is how you responded.

## What should you do in the first few minutes?

1. **Check the pet and yourself first.** If glass is broken, a pet could be hurt. Move the animal to a safe room and check paws before anything else.
2. **Make the area safe.** Sweep up sharp pieces, keep pets away from anything harmful, and turn off an appliance or water if that is part of the problem. If it is an emergency such as a flood or a fire, follow your emergency plan and call the right service.
3. **Stop and breathe.** Do not rush to hide or fix it in a way that makes things worse, such as scrubbing a stain with a product that could set it.
4. **Do not throw anything away yet.** Keep broken pieces in a safe place, because the owner may want to see them or try to repair the item.

## How do you document what happened?

Take clear photos before and after you clean up, and write a short note while it is fresh. Include:

- The date and time.
- What was damaged and where.
- What happened, in plain words, without guessing.
- What you did immediately afterward.
- Whether the pet was involved and whether the animal was hurt.

A factual note helps everyone. It lets you explain the situation clearly, and it gives an insurer, if you have one, what they will ask for. Stick to observation. "The glass vase on the hall table fell when Rex pulled the leash around the corner" is a fact. "Rex has bad manners" is an opinion that does not help anyone.

## When should you tell the owner?

As soon as is reasonable, and in your own words, not through a vague line in the daily update. For small things like a cracked mug, a short message the same day is usually right. For larger damage, call first, then follow up in writing. Do not wait for them to find it. If you are waiting for an owner on a trip, send a clear message and tell them you will keep them updated.

Delay makes it worse. A sitter who says "I need to tell you something that happened this afternoon" feels trustworthy. A sitter who says nothing, and is found out later, feels like a risk.

## What should you say?

Keep it short, factual, and sincere. A message might read:

"I wanted to tell you right away that I broke a glass vase on the hall table during this afternoon's visit. The leash caught it as Rex came around the corner. Rex is fine and I checked his paws. I have cleaned up the glass and kept the larger pieces in a bag by the door in case you would like to see them. I am sorry, and I would like to make it right. Please let me know how you would like to handle it."

A good message does five things:

- States what happened.
- Confirms the pet is safe.
- Says what you have done.
- Takes responsibility without over-explaining.
- Asks the owner how they would like to proceed.

Avoid long excuses, blaming the pet, or minimizing ("it was just an old vase"). You do not know what the item means to the owner.

## How do you make it right?

Let the owner lead, but come with options. Common ways to resolve a small accident include replacing the item, paying for a repair or cleaning, or offering a credit on a future visit. Ask what they prefer. Before you agree to anything large, think about whether it is something your insurance covers and whether your service agreement says anything about damage. If you do not have a written agreement, this is a good reason to add a short section about accidents and damages to your next one.

Put what you agree on in writing, even if it is a one-line text: "Confirming that I will replace the vase by Friday." That prevents misunderstandings later.

## What if you are not sure whether you caused it?

Sometimes you notice a stain or a scratch and cannot say where it came from. Describe it, do not guess, and tell the owner. "I noticed a small scratch on the wooden floor near the back door at today's visit. I am not sure how it happened, but I wanted to tell you and I have taken a photo." That is honest and protects you if the owner notices it later and wonders whether it was there before. It also shows why a quick photo walkthrough at the first visit can be useful, as long as the owner has agreed to it.

## What if the pet caused the damage?

If a dog chewed a pillow or a cat knocked something off a shelf, the owner will probably still see the home as your responsibility during that visit, so tell them in the same way. It may be useful to describe what you saw, say what you did to prevent more, and ask whether the pet has done something like this before. Their answer may help both of you plan the next visit.

## How can you reduce the chance of damage?

You cannot prevent every accident, but some habits help:

- **Ask about fragile items** at the meet and greet and move them if the owner agrees.
- **Note where the pet is calm and where it gets excited**, such as near a door or a window.
- **Keep leashes short** in narrow hallways and around furniture.
- **Do a quick visual check** on arrival and when you leave.
- **Give yourself time** so you are not rushing between visits.
- **Know what your insurance covers.** Many professional sitters carry liability insurance, but terms differ, so ask your provider what applies to you.

## How does this fit into your visit notes?

If something happens, record it in the visit note as a plain, factual line, and keep the longer discussion in a separate message to the owner. Your routine update should not hide it, and it should not turn into a long apology either. Honest, steady updates for the rest of the stay show the client that the incident was handled.

## A short checklist for the moment it happens

- Is the pet safe, and am I safe?
- Is the area safe?
- Have I taken photos and written down what happened?
- Have I told the owner promptly and plainly?
- Did I ask how they would like to resolve it?
- Did I write down what we agreed?
- Is there something I can change so it is less likely to happen again?

Accidents are part of working in real homes. If you respond honestly and calmly, an accident can become the moment a client learns that you can be trusted when things go wrong.
