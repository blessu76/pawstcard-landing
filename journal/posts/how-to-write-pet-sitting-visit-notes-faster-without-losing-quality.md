# How to Write Pet Sitting Visit Notes Faster, Without Losing Quality

**The fastest way to write good visit notes is to capture a few facts during the visit, use the same simple structure every time, and send the update before you lock the door.** Often the problem is not that sitters write too much. It is that they try to remember the visit later, start from a blank page, and rewrite sentences they have written a hundred times.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers. Every business is different, so treat it as a starting point and change whatever does not fit your clients.

## Why do visit notes take so long?

Writing a note three hours after a visit means you are reconstructing it from memory. You wonder whether the dog drank water at the first or second stop, whether you wrote about the new toy yesterday, and whether you remembered to mention the treat. Each uncertainty adds a minute.

There are usually three hidden time costs:

- **Remembering.** Trying to recall details after other visits have crowded them out.
- **Starting.** Staring at a blank message and deciding how to open it.
- **Polishing.** Rewriting a perfectly good sentence because it feels flat.

You can reduce all three without making your updates colder or shorter than clients like.

## What should you capture during the visit?

You do not need to write full sentences at the door. You need quick facts that you can turn into a note later. A small, consistent set works well:

- **Time in and out.**
- **Food and water:** what was eaten, what was left, whether fresh water was given.
- **Bathroom:** yes or no, and anything unusual.
- **Activity:** where you walked, what you played, how long.
- **Mood and energy:** one or two words, such as "relaxed," "bouncy," or "a little quiet."
- **Anything different from normal:** a limp, a skipped meal, a new smell, something moved in the house.
- **One photo** that shows the pet and, ideally, something about the visit.

Use whatever is fastest for you. Some sitters tap a few quick notes into their phone, some dictate a voice memo in the car, and some use a checklist. The best method is the one you will actually use on a busy day. If you are working through a stack of visits, the habit matters more than the tool.

## How do you build a simple template?

A template is not a form letter. It is an order of information that you fill in with real details. A four-part structure is easy to remember:

1. **Arrival and mood.** "Arrived at 12:05. Luna was waiting by the door and very excited."
2. **What we did.** "We walked the usual loop, about 30 minutes, and she sniffed at every bush."
3. **Food, water, and bathroom.** "Ate her whole lunch, drank well, and went twice."
4. **Anything to know.** "She is scratching her ear a little more than yesterday. I did not see anything wrong. Wanted you to know."

Write the opening line in a way that suits the pet. "Luna was waiting by the door" is better than "The visit went well." A client who is away from home wants to picture their pet, so one specific sentence is worth more than three general ones.

If you do not like writing, you can keep a small library of phrases you actually say: ways to describe a good walk, a quiet afternoon, a hesitant eater. Copying your own wording is fine as long as you add a real detail from the day. Clients notice when a note is identical to yesterday's, so change at least one line every time.

## When should you send it?

Send the update as soon as the visit ends, ideally before you leave the property or just after you get back in your car. The details are fresh, the photo is on your phone, and you do not carry unfinished notes into the rest of your day. A note that takes two minutes at the door can easily take far longer at night.

If a visit has a problem, such as an accident, a skipped meal, or a worry about the pet, write that one thoughtfully and separately, even if it takes longer. Speed is for routine updates, not for hard conversations.

## How many photos and how much text are enough?

For most routine visits, one clear photo and four to six sentences is plenty. Owners usually want to know three things: Is my pet okay? Did the visit happen as planned? Is there anything I need to do? Anything beyond that is a bonus, not an obligation. If a client asks for more detail, you can add it for that client without changing your process for everyone.

If you are not sure what clients want, ask in the first week. "Is this the right amount of detail, or would you like more or less?" takes thirty seconds and can save you months of overwriting.

## How can you batch work without sacrificing honesty?

Batching is helpful, but do not batch the facts. It is fine to review all your notes in the evening and tidy typos. It is not fine to write several visits from memory and guess at the details. Capture the facts at each visit, then use the evening, if you want one, for light editing and for catching anything missed. If you notice a gap, say so plainly or leave the detail out. An honest short note is better than a polished one that is partly invented.

## What about back-to-back visits?

When visits are close together, build the note into the travel time. Before you pull out of the driveway, spend one minute on the update. If you cannot finish before the next appointment, send a quick "All good here, full note and photo coming this evening," and then do exactly that. This also stops you from carrying a mental list of unfinished updates all day.

## How do you keep quality from slipping?

Speed becomes a problem when notes are empty. A short check helps before you send:

- Is there at least one detail that could only come from this visit?
- Did I mention food, water, and bathroom, or say why I did not?
- Did I flag anything unusual?
- Does it sound like me?

If the answer is yes to all four, a three-minute note is a good note. If you find yourself always skipping one of them, add it to your capture list so it is not a decision at the end of the day.

## A simple routine you can try this week

1. Pick your capture list of five to seven facts and keep it somewhere you can see it.
2. Choose a four-part structure and use it for every routine visit.
3. Take one good photo at every visit.
4. Send the update before you leave or as soon as you are in the car.
5. For a problem visit, take the extra time and write it separately.
6. Once a week, look at your notes. Which lines do clients reply to? Keep those. Drop what nobody reads.

After a week or two, many sitters find that routine notes get noticeably quicker, and clients still get the detail they care about. Updates that arrive on time and cover the same basics every day are easy to trust.
