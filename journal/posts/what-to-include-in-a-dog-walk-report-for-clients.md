# What to Include in a Dog Walk Report for Clients

**A good dog walk report says when the walk happened, how long it lasted, what the dog did, how the dog behaved, and whether anything needs the owner's attention, all in a few lines and ideally with one photo.** Clients are away from home when you walk their dog, so the report is how they know the walk happened and went well. It does not need to be long. It needs to be specific and consistent.

This is general guidance for independent dog walkers and sitters. Follow any agreement you have with each client and any local rules that apply to walking dogs where you work.

## Why do clients want a walk report at all?

The owner is at work or traveling. They want to know the walk really took place, that the dog is safe, and that the time was used well. A report also gives them useful information about their dog, such as appetite, energy, and mood, which they can compare with how the dog acts at home.

For you, a report is a record. If a question comes up later, such as whether the dog drank water or had a loose stool, your notes give you something to check.

## What belongs in the report?

Think of it as a short checklist:

1. **Time and length.** Start and end times, or at least how long the walk was.
2. **Route or place.** A park, a trail, or "around the neighborhood." Enough detail for the owner to picture it, without sharing more than the client wants.
3. **Potty breaks.** Whether the dog peed or pooped, and anything unusual.
4. **Behavior.** Calm, excited, nervous, pulling, a new friend, a reaction to another dog.
5. **Water and food.** If you gave water or the dog ate a treat or meal.
6. **Anything to flag.** A limp, a sore paw, a scratched ear, a tick, signs of overheating, or a close call.
7. **One photo,** if photos are part of the arrangement.

## How long should it be?

Aim for three to five short lines. If you find yourself writing a paragraph for every walk, the client will stop reading, and you will stop wanting to write. Save the longer note for something that deserves it.

A short sample:

> Hi Jordan, Bailey and I walked for 40 minutes around the park loop. Two pee stops and one poop, normal. She was bouncy at the start and calmer by the end. She had a drink at the fountain and loved sniffing around the big oak tree. Photo attached!

## How do you report problems without alarming people?

State what you saw, what you did, and what you suggest. Keep the tone calm and factual.

- **What you saw:** "Bailey was licking her back paw after the walk."
- **What you did:** "I checked it and saw no cut or swelling. I wiped her paws and she stopped."
- **What you suggest:** "You may want to take a look tonight."

You are not diagnosing. You are passing along an observation so the owner can decide what to do. If something seems urgent, call or message right away and follow the plan you agreed on instead of waiting for the end-of-walk update.

## What should you leave out?

- **Private details** about the home, such as alarm codes or exact schedules, in messages that could be forwarded
- **Faces or license plates of other people** in photos, when you can avoid it
- **Guesses about health,** which are better described as observations
- **Judgments about other dogs or owners,** since these can get awkward quickly
- **Excuses.** If a walk was shorter than planned, say why plainly.

## How do you stay consistent without burning out?

Consistency matters more than detail. Pick a simple pattern and use it for every client, such as time, length, potty, behavior, one flag if needed, and one photo. When the pattern is stable, writing the report takes a minute or two, and clients learn where to find what they want.

Some walkers send the report right after the walk while it is fresh. Others batch their notes during a break. Either works, as long as clients get the update on the day, not days later.

Finally, vary the little details. A small, true moment, like a new squirrel the dog tried to befriend, is the part clients remember.
