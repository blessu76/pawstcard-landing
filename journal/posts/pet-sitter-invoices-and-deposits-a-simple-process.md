# Pet Sitter Invoices and Deposits Made Simple

**A simple invoicing process has four parts: say clearly in writing when payment is due, send an invoice that lists exactly what the client is paying for, keep a record of what has been paid, and follow up politely and on schedule when something is late.** You do not need accounting software to start. A consistent routine that clients understand from the beginning is more important than the tool you use.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Rules about deposits, taxes, and payment records differ by place, so check what applies where you work and talk to an accountant about taxes.

## Why does a clear payment process matter?

Money conversations feel awkward for many sitters, especially when the client is friendly and the pet is adorable. A clear process takes the personal feeling out of it. When everyone knows when payment is due and how to pay, you do not have to improvise, and the client does not have to guess.

A tidy invoice also reflects well on your business. It tells the client that you keep good records and take the job seriously.

## What should an invoice include?

Keep it short and complete. A good invoice lists:

- **Your name or business name and contact details**
- **The client's name** and the pet's name
- **An invoice number or date,** so you can find it later
- **The dates and services:** for example, "Drop-in visits, Nov 3 to Nov 7" or "Two overnights"
- **The rate for each service,** written as $X per visit or $Y per night, using your own figures
- **Any extra charges,** such as a holiday date or an additional pet, listed on their own line
- **The total amount due and the due date**
- **How to pay**

Itemizing the extras helps avoid surprises. If you agreed to an extra charge in writing earlier, you are only repeating what the client already knows.

## What is a deposit, and should you ask for one?

A deposit is an amount paid in advance to hold the dates. Some sitters ask for one, especially for holidays and longer stays, when turning down other bookings has a real cost. Others do not. Both can work.

If you ask for a deposit, say so in your service agreement and answer a few questions in writing:

- How much is it, and when is it due?
- Is it applied to the final total?
- Is it refundable if the client cancels, and under what conditions?
- What happens to it if you have to cancel?

Rules about deposits and refunds can differ by location. If you are unsure, ask a local professional before you set your terms.

## When should you send the invoice?

Pick a pattern and stick with it. Common options are:

1. **Before the job.** The client pays in full or in part before you start. This is simple for short jobs.
2. **After each visit or each week.** Useful for regular dog walking.
3. **After the job ends.** Works for one-off stays, as long as the due date is clear.

Whichever you choose, tell the client at the meet-and-greet and repeat it in your agreement. For regular clients, a monthly invoice on a fixed date can reduce admin work.

## How do you keep records?

A spreadsheet is enough. Make a row for each invoice with the date, client, amount, due date, and the date paid. Save copies of invoices and any payment confirmations in a folder. Good records help at tax time and make it easier to answer a client who asks, "Did I already pay for last week?"

Do not rely on memory or message threads. Write it down.

## What if a payment is late?

Stay calm and professional. Send a brief reminder soon after the due date:

> Hi Sam, a quick reminder that the invoice for Nov 3 to Nov 7 was due on Nov 10. Could you let me know when I can expect payment? Thanks so much.

If there is still no reply, follow up again after a few days, and refer to the terms the client agreed to. You can also pause future bookings until the invoice is settled if your agreement says so. Keep the tone factual and kind. A late payment may simply be an oversight.

If a payment issue becomes serious, ask a local professional about your options rather than guessing.

## Should you handle money during visits?

Some sitters prefer not to take cash at the door. Paying by a method you can record is easier to track and reduces mix-ups. Whatever you choose, give the client a clear, written way to pay.
