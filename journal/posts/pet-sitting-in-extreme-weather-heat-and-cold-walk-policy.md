# Pet Sitting in Extreme Weather: A Heat and Cold Walk Policy

**Write a short weather policy before you need it: say what conditions might change or shorten a walk, what you will do instead, and how you will tell the client, then follow it the same way every time.** Heat, cold, ice, and storms can make a normal walk risky for some dogs. A plain policy protects the animals, protects your schedule, and saves you from making stressful decisions at the door.

This is general guidance for independent sitters and dog walkers, not veterinary advice. Pets differ by breed, age, coat, and health, so follow the owner's instructions and your vet's guidance, and check local weather alerts and rules where you work.

## Why do you need a weather policy?

Weather decisions tend to arrive at the worst time: a hot afternoon with a full schedule, or an icy morning when the client is already at work. Without a plan, you may feel pressure to walk anyway, or you may cancel in a way that surprises the client.

A written policy makes the answer clear in advance. Clients know what to expect, and you do not have to negotiate every time.

## What should the policy cover?

Keep it to a page or less. A good policy answers a handful of questions:

1. **What counts as unsafe for walks?** Describe it in your own words, such as "very hot afternoons," "icy sidewalks," or "thunderstorms." You can also say that you use your judgment and the owner's notes about their pet.
2. **What happens instead?** A shorter walk, a walk at a cooler or warmer time, indoor play, or a potty-only trip.
3. **Who decides?** Say that you make the final call for safety, after considering the pet and the conditions.
4. **How will you tell the client?** A message before the visit, if possible, and a note in the update afterward.
5. **Are fees affected?** State whether a changed visit is charged normally, adjusted, or rescheduled.
6. **What if the client disagrees?** Explain that you will not walk when you believe it is unsafe.

## How do you think about hot weather?

Dogs cool themselves differently than people do, and some are more at risk than others, including dogs with short noses, thick coats, or health issues. Hot pavement can also hurt paws. Common choices sitters make include:

- **Moving walks** to early morning or later evening
- **Shortening walks** and choosing shade
- **Bringing water** and offering it often
- **Choosing grass** over hot pavement
- **Watching the dog closely** for signs of trouble, and stopping early

If you see signs of overheating, stop, move to shade, offer water, and contact the owner right away, following the emergency plan you agreed on. Describe what you saw rather than guessing at a cause.

## How do you think about cold, ice, and snow?

Cold affects small dogs, short coats, seniors, and some medical conditions more strongly. Ice makes slips more likely for both you and the dog. Useful steps include:

- **Shortening the walk** and staying close to home
- **Using the coat, boots, or gear** the owner provides
- **Wiping paws** after the walk, since salt and ice melt can irritate
- **Watching your footing,** and skipping steps or hills that are slick
- **Checking that the dog warms up** properly when you return

Some dogs love snow. Even so, in very cold weather a shorter, careful outing is often the safer choice.

## What about storms, smoke, and other conditions?

Lightning, strong wind, and heavy rain can be scary and dangerous. Poor air quality, alerts, and floods also change plans. You do not need a separate rule for each one. Your policy can say that you may change or skip walks during severe weather or official alerts, and that you will offer another time or an indoor visit when you can.

Also think about nervous dogs. Some pets are frightened of thunder, and a calm indoor visit may be better than a walk.

## How do you tell clients about a change?

Send the message as early as you can, and keep it short and kind:

> Hi Priya, it's going to be very hot this afternoon, so I'd like to walk Rufus a bit earlier, around 11, and keep it shorter. If that doesn't work, I can do a quick potty break and some indoor play instead. I'll send an update afterward.

Afterward, say what you actually did, such as walk length, water, and how the dog seemed. That closes the loop and shows you took the weather seriously.

## How do you keep the policy fair?

Apply it the same way for every client. Mention it when you first onboard someone, and again in your service terms. If you update it, tell existing clients. Over time, notes from past weather days can help you refine what works.
