# What to Send a New Pet Sitting Client After the First Visit

**After a new client's first visit, send a short, warm update the same day that says how the visit went, mentions one specific detail about their pet, asks one useful question, and makes the next step easy.** The first message after the first visit tells a new client what working with you will feel like. A clear, friendly note often does more for trust than any amount of explaining beforehand.

This is general guidance for independent sitters and dog walkers. Follow any agreement you have with the client and any rules that apply where you work.

## Why does the first follow-up matter so much?

A new client has handed you their pet, and often their home, on the strength of a conversation or a meet-and-greet. They are curious and a little nervous. Whatever you send first sets the tone for every update after it.

Silence, or a one-word reply, leaves room for worry. A thoughtful note shows that you pay attention. It also gives you a natural moment to confirm details you may have guessed at during the visit.

## When should you send it?

Send the first follow-up as soon as you can after the visit, ideally within the hour, and certainly the same day. If the visit ran long or something unexpected happened, still send a short message and promise more detail later.

Do not wait until you have a perfect message. A simple, honest note sent on time beats a polished one that arrives the next morning.

## What should the first message include?

Think of it as a small checklist:

1. **A greeting that uses the pet's name.** "Milo was so sweet today" feels personal.
2. **What you did.** Fed, walked, played, gave fresh water, or whatever the visit covered.
3. **One specific moment.** A detail only someone present would know, such as which toy he picked or how he settled by the window.
4. **Anything worth flagging,** stated calmly: a skipped meal, a strong pull on the leash, a worry about a door or gate.
5. **A photo or two,** if the client has agreed to photo updates.
6. **One question or next step.**

A short example:

> Hi Dana, Milo did great today. We had a good walk around the block, he finished his lunch, and he picked the blue rope toy for a quick game of tug. One thing I noticed: he seemed to prefer the shaded side of the street. See you Thursday!

## What should you ask a new client?

Keep it to one question so the message does not feel like a form. Good choices include:

- "Is there anything you'd like me to do differently next time?"
- "Would you like a photo every visit, or just a note?"
- "I noticed X. Is that normal for her?"

Asking a real question tells the client you are listening, and their answer helps you tailor your updates from the start. If you need several answers, space them out over the first few visits instead of sending a list at once.

## How do you handle a problem on the first visit?

If something went wrong, such as a missed dose, a spilled bowl, or a pet who would not come to you, say so plainly in the same message. State what happened, what you did about it, and what you will do next time. Do not hide it, and do not over-apologize.

Clients tend to respond well to honesty and a clear plan. Learning about a problem later, from someone else, damages trust far more than a calm note up front.

## How do you set up the next booking?

End with a light next step instead of a sales pitch:

- Confirm the next scheduled visit, if there is one.
- Offer to set up a regular schedule if they have mentioned wanting one.
- Share how to reach you for changes.

If you have not yet agreed on a routine, you might add: "Let me know if you'd like me to put you down for the same time next week." That gives them an easy yes without pressure.

## What should you avoid?

- **Long messages** that read like a report. Save detail for when it is useful.
- **Generic phrases** that could describe any pet. Specifics are what clients remember.
- **Asking for a review right away.** Wait until you have built some history.
- **Promising more than you can do,** such as daily photos you cannot sustain.

Pick a standard you can keep up for every client, then keep it.
