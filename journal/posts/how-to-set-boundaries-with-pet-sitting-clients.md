# How to Set Boundaries With Pet Sitting Clients

**Boundaries are not about being strict. They are about telling clients, early and kindly, how your service works so that everyone knows what to expect.** Independent pet sitters often work from their phones, and without clear limits a business can easily spill into evenings, weekends, and every free moment.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not legal or business advice.

## Why do boundaries matter for a pet sitter?

Pet care is personal. Clients become friendly, and friendly clients may message you late at night or ask for small favors that were never part of the agreement. Each request seems small. Together they can cause you to feel tired and resentful, which can affect the quality of your care. Clear boundaries protect your time and keep your clients' trust.

## Which boundaries are worth setting?

Think about the areas where confusion happens most:

- **Contact hours.** When do you answer messages? When do you not?
- **Response times.** Do you reply within a few hours, or by the next morning?
- **Emergencies.** What counts as urgent, and how should a client reach you?
- **Scope of service.** Which tasks are included and which are extra, such as watering plants or collecting mail?
- **Schedule changes.** How much notice do you need for a new request?
- **Pets and homes.** Which rooms and tasks are in your agreement?

You do not need to set every boundary at once. Start with the ones that cause you the most stress.

## When is the best time to set them?

Before the first visit. The conversation at the meet and greet and the written agreement are natural places to explain how you work. Saying "I answer messages between 8 am and 7 pm" at the start sounds professional. Saying it for the first time after a late night message can sound like a complaint.

## How do you say it kindly?

Be warm, specific, and positive. Instead of "Do not message me after 7," try "I check messages between 8 am and 7 pm so I can give each client my full attention. If something urgent comes up in the evening, please call me." Explaining the benefit to the client makes the boundary feel like part of the service, not a restriction.

## What should you do when a client crosses a boundary?

Stay calm, and respond once with a friendly reminder. If a client asks for something outside your scope, you can say, "I would be glad to help with that. It is an extra task, so here is how I would add it to the schedule." If you do not want to do it, say so politely. "I am not able to do that, but I can suggest another option" is a complete answer.

Do not give in repeatedly and then become frustrated later. If a boundary has been crossed several times, update the agreement or have a short, kind conversation.

## How can written updates help?

Predictable updates can reduce the number of messages you receive. When clients know they will get a short note and photo after every visit, they have less reason to ask "How is she doing?" at random times. Tell them when to expect the update, such as "within an hour after each visit," and then do it consistently.

## What about saying no?

You are allowed to decline requests, clients, or time slots that do not suit you. Saying no early, with a short and friendly explanation, is easier on everyone than saying yes and doing the work reluctantly. A client who respects your boundaries is often a good fit for the long term.
