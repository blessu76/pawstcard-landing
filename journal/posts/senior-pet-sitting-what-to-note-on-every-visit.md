# Senior Pet Sitting: What to Note on Every Visit

**When you sit for an older pet, the most useful thing you can do is observe carefully, write down what you see in plain words, and pass anything unusual to the owner so they can decide whether to call their vet.** Older animals can change gradually, and a sitter who visits regularly may notice small shifts that a busy owner misses. You are not there to diagnose. You are there to be a reliable pair of eyes and a clear communicator.

This is general guidance for independent pet sitters and dog walkers, not veterinary advice. Follow the owner's and the vet's instructions, and suggest that the owner consult their vet about any health concern.

## What is different about caring for a senior pet?

Older pets often have routines that matter more than they do for younger animals. They may move more slowly, need more rest, take medication, or have special food. Some are comfortable in the same spot day after day and dislike changes. Others need extra help with stairs, slippery floors, or getting in and out of the car.

Because every animal is different, do not assume what is normal for a senior pet. Ask the owner.

## What should you ask the owner before you start?

Treat the first conversation as a short interview about this particular pet. Useful questions include:

- What does a normal day look like? When does the pet eat, drink, rest, and go outside?
- Are there medications, supplements, or special foods? How are they given?
- How does the pet move around the home? Are there stairs, ramps, or rugs to be aware of?
- How long a walk is comfortable, and when should you cut it short?
- What does the pet do when relaxed, and what does it do when something is bothering it?
- Which vet should you contact, and what are the owner's wishes in an emergency?
- Is there anything the owner is currently watching, such as a change in appetite?

Write the answers on a one-page sheet you can read before each visit.

## What should you observe on every visit?

Describe what you see rather than interpreting it. Useful categories are:

- **Appetite and drinking.** Did the pet eat all, part, or none of the meal? Did the water level change?
- **Bathroom habits.** Frequency, effort, or accidents inside the home, noted in simple terms.
- **Movement.** How the pet stood up, walked, or climbed, and anything that looked different from the owner's description.
- **Energy and mood.** Alert, sleepy, restless, or hiding.
- **Breathing and comfort,** as you observe them, such as whether the pet settled easily.
- **Medication.** What was given, when, and how the pet took it.

A short entry such as "Ate about half the breakfast, drank normally, took a slow ten-minute walk and rested twice" is more helpful than "doing okay."

## When should you tell the owner something right away?

Use your agreement and the owner's instructions as your guide. In general, tell the owner promptly when you notice something that is new, sudden, or different from what they described. Examples include a pet refusing food, struggling to stand, showing distress, or having an accident that is out of the ordinary.

Say what you observed and when, and say that you are letting them know so they can decide whether to contact their vet. If you believe the pet is in urgent trouble, follow the emergency plan the owner gave you and contact the vet or emergency clinic they named. Do not guess at causes or suggest treatments.

## How can you make visits more comfortable?

Small adjustments often help. Move slowly and speak calmly. Keep to the owner's routine. Let the pet set the pace on walks, and cut them short if the owner has asked you to or if the pet seems tired. Keep water within easy reach. Be careful with slippery floors, and follow the owner's instructions about helping the pet on and off furniture or stairs.

If a pet is nervous around new people, give them a moment to approach you instead of reaching out first.

## How should you update the client?

Older pets often have worried owners, so a short, steady update can be reassuring. Include one photo, a sentence about eating and drinking, a sentence about movement or mood, and any medication given. If everything was normal, say so plainly. If something was different, describe it and ask if they would like you to do anything.

Consistency matters more than length. A client who gets the same type of update each time quickly learns to notice when something changes.

## What about the hard moments?

Caring for a senior pet sometimes means being part of difficult times. Follow the owner's wishes, be kind, and avoid giving opinions on medical decisions. If you have serious or sad news to share, try to reach the owner by phone first rather than by text, unless they have told you they prefer another way. It helps to ask at the start of the job how they would want to hear difficult news.
